# Website spec: the 0-to-1 discovery sprint (v3)

Information architecture, page copy, and user flows. No visual design.

This version keeps the customer-first structure from v2 and adds the productization layer: outcomes framed as assets, a clear "you won't need me" promise, honest proof, gentle selectivity, and the continuation offer positioned as a natural next step rather than an upsell.

Still not a sales page. The goal is a trust magnet.

---

## Positioning summary (read this first)

**Model: productized sprint.** Fixed scope, fixed price, clean start and end, no hourly anything.

**What's being sold:** not building. Judgment about what to build, plus the ability to make it real fast. In a market where anyone can generate an app, the scarce thing is knowing what deserves to exist and what to cut. That's the whole position.

**The promise that makes it premium:** you leave able to decide without me. Not dependent, not on a retainer, not needing another sprint to understand your own product.

**Buyer:** a non-technical founder or domain expert who has an idea, can't build it themselves, and wants a straight answer rather than a yes-man. Usually someone who has been burned before, or is analytical enough to want a reality check.

---

## What changed from v2, and why

- **Deliverables are now named assets, not activities.** "I build a prototype and write a read" becomes "you get the tested build and the decision brief." Named things feel like property. Activities feel like hours.
- **Added an explicit "you won't need me" promise.** Counterintuitive, but it's the strongest trust signal available and it's true to how you work.
- **Added a short "who this is for / not for" section.** Gentle selectivity. It filters badly-matched leads and signals that you choose your work.
- **The continuation offer is now framed as a natural next step,** available if it makes sense, never as the point of the sprint.
- **Proof is reframed honestly.** See the warning below.

### Warning: one constraint in the reference is a trap

The reference suggests claiming methods "refined through hundreds of successful client engagements." **Do not write that.** You have 20 years of product and design practice and nine self-built apps. That is real and it is enough. Inventing a client-volume claim would poison the exact thing this whole position rests on, which is that you tell the truth even when it costs you. The honest version is stronger anyway: "the judgment comes from 20 years of deciding what to build. The speed comes from nine apps built in the last few months."

---

## Ground rules for copy

- Headlines, headings, titles in sentence case.
- Never use the em dash. Use periods, commas, colons, or parentheses.
- Voice: conversational American. Plain, direct, a little dry. Talks to one person.
- Center "you," not "I." Where a sentence wants to start with "I," check if it can describe the customer's experience instead.
- Never describe effort ("I spend the week building"). Describe what they end up owning ("you own a working build").

---

## The one thing this site has to do

Get a non-technical founder with an idea and a quiet fear it might be wrong to book one low-friction call.

---

## Sitemap

```
Home  (customer-first pitch, long scroll, most visitors never leave it)
 ├─ How it works        (the deep process, the decision rule, what comes after)
 ├─ What I've built     (proof: apps reframed as judgment)
 ├─ About               (secondary: the person, the story, the 20 years)
 └─ Start               (the booking / intro-call page, the one conversion point)
```

The primary CTA ("Book a free intro call") appears on every page, always pointing to **Start**. About is where all "about me" content lives, kept out of the primary flow.

## Page 1: Home

### Section 1: Hero

**Job:** pass the 5-second test. What problem, what life looks like after, how to start.

Headline options:

> ### Know whether your idea is worth building, before you spend months finding out.

> ### Test the risky part of your idea in two weeks, not two quarters.

> ### Find out if people want your idea, before you bet real money on it.

Subheadline:

> In two weeks you own a working version of the one part that matters most, evidence from real users, and a straight answer: keep going, or stop.
>
> [ Book a free intro call ]

Small line under the button:

> A short, no-pressure call about your idea and whether this fits.

### Section 2: The problem, named once

**Job:** empathy. Name the bind, then move on. Do not wallow.

> ## The scary part isn't building it. It's building the wrong thing.
> Your idea has probably been sitting in a doc for a while. Not because you're lazy, but because committing real money to it feels like a leap and you can't quite see where you'd land.
>
> That instinct is right. Most ideas don't fail because nobody could build them. They fail because someone spent months and a pile of money before finding out that nobody wanted the thing that got built.

### Section 3: You might be thinking

**Job:** mirror the doubts they haven't said out loud.

> ## You might be thinking
>
> "What if it's a bad idea and I find out too late."
> You find out first, before anything gets built.
>
> "I'm not technical. I won't understand what's happening."
> You don't need to be. You'll get it explained clearly enough to decide with confidence.
>
> "I don't want to be talked into a build I don't need."
> You won't be. You'll get an honest answer even when the honest answer is no.

### Section 4: What your life looks like after

**Job:** the result. This is what they're buying: the feeling of knowing.

> ## In two weeks, you go from guessing to knowing
> Instead of a doc full of hope and a knot in your stomach, you have something real in your hands and evidence about whether it works.
>
> You can walk into a room and show it, not describe it. You can point to what real users did with it, not what you hope they'd do. And you can make the next call, spend or stop, with your eyes open.
>
> If it's a go, you know exactly what to build next. If it's a stop, you kept your money and a year of your life.

### Section 5: How it works (the process roadmap)

**Job:** a simple three-step map. One of the strongest trust signals available.

> ## Three steps, start to answer
>
> **1. Find out if it's worth building.**
> You bring the idea. Together you find the one thing that has to be true for it to work, and you get a straight call. If there's a fatal flaw, you find it here, cheaply, before anything gets built.
>
> **2. Get a real version of the part that matters.**
> If it's worth building, you get one working flow (the core of the idea) that real people can actually use. Not a mockup. Something you can put in front of users.
>
> **3. Test it and decide, with a clear rule.**
> Before testing, you agree what result means go, what means adjust, what means stop. Then you test, and you decide with evidence instead of a gut feeling.
>
> [ See how it works in detail ]

Build note: render as a visual three-step map so the path reads at a glance.

### Section 6: What you own at the end

**Job:** make the outcome concrete and ownable. Named assets, not activities.

> ## What you own at the end
>
> **The working build.** One real flow through the core of your idea, usable on a phone. Yours to keep, extend, or hand to any team.
>
> **The decision brief.** What the riskiest assumption was, what the testing showed, and a straight call: go, adjust, or stop.
>
> **The testing playbook.** How to find the right people, how to run sessions so politeness doesn't pollute the results, and what to watch for. Reusable on every idea you have after this one.
>
> That last one matters more than it looks. It's the part that keeps working long after the sprint ends.

### Section 7: You shouldn't need me twice

**Job:** the premium trust move. Promise independence, not dependence.

> ## The goal is that you don't need me again
> Plenty of people in this business are quietly hoping you'll stay. This works the other way round.
>
> The sprint is built so that when it ends, you can read your own evidence, make your own call, and either move forward or walk away without another opinion. You keep the build, the brief, and the method.
>
> If you want to keep working together after that, good. But it should be because you chose to, not because you got left halfway.

### Section 8: Why you can trust the answer

**Job:** competence, framed as the customer's benefit. No career history here.

> ## Why you can trust the answer
>
> **You get judgment, not just a build.** Anyone can generate an app now. The hard part is knowing what to make and what to cut, and that's the part you're paying for.
>
> **You get advice from someone who isn't paid to say yes.** When the honest answer is "don't build this," you'll hear it. That's only possible because the value here is the clarity, not the code.
>
> **You get speed without losing the thinking.** The idea gets real in days, with a designer's read on how people actually behave built in.
>
> The judgment comes from 20 years of deciding what to build and what to cut. The speed comes from nine working apps built in the last few months. If you want that story, it's on the [about page].

### Section 9: Proof this is real

> ## Proof this is real
> Nine working apps, built for real ideas, using the same tools and the same speed you'd get. Each one is a small lesson in what to build and what to cut.
>
> [ See what I've built and why ]

### Section 10: Who this is for

**Job:** gentle selectivity. Filters bad fits, signals that the work is chosen, not chased. Keep it warm, not haughty.

> ## Who this works for
>
> **This fits if** you have an idea you believe in but can't build yourself, you'd rather hear a hard truth early than a comfortable one late, and you're ready to put something in front of real people in the next month or two.
>
> **This probably isn't it if** you already know exactly what you want built and just need hands, if you need a finished, scalable product rather than something to learn from, or if you'd rather not hear that the idea has a problem.
>
> No hard feelings either way. A quick call sorts it out in twenty minutes.

### Section 11: How pricing works

> ## How pricing works
>
> **Step one (is this worth building?) is a small fixed price,** set so it's worth it even when the answer is "don't build."
>
> **Step two (the build) is scoped after step one,** once you both know what's actually needed. No surprise numbers, no big commitment before you have proof.
>
> **Prefer not to run the testing yourself?** That can be added on.
>
> You'll see the whole staircase before you take the first step.

### Section 12: If you want to keep going

**Job:** the continuation offer as a natural next step, never as the point.

> ## If you want to keep going
> Some ideas pass the test and want to grow. If that's yours, the work can continue in short cycles while the product is still finding its shape.
>
> And when it grows past what one person should handle, you'll hear that too, plus help handing the heavy build to the right team.
>
> None of this is decided up front. The sprint stands on its own.

### Section 13: Questions people ask

> ## Questions people ask
>
> **What if you tell me my idea is bad?**
> Then you've saved months and a fortune, and you'll get a clear reason why, not a vague no.
>
> **Do I need to understand the tech?**
> No. You need to understand your customer. The rest is handled.
>
> **Who owns what gets built?**
> You do. It's yours to take anywhere.
>
> **Is this a full product?**
> No, and that's the point. It's the smallest real thing that answers the biggest question. Building the full product before that answer is exactly the expensive mistake this avoids.
>
> **What if I already know it's a good idea?**
> Then step one is quick and you'll have saved nothing but a little time. Most people who are certain turn out to be certain about the wrong part.

### Section 14: Final call to action

> ## Let's find out if your idea is real
> One call. You talk through your idea, you hear honestly whether this fits. No pitch, no pressure.
>
> [ Book a free intro call ]

## Page 2: How it works

**Job:** the full process for the reader who needs it before they trust it.

1. **Intro.** "Here's exactly what happens, so there are no surprises for you."
2. **Step one in full.** What the qualifying questions probe: a real painful problem vs. a feature that sounds cool, a reachable customer vs. "everyone," "I'd use it" vs. "people will pay," whether the exciting part is actually the risky part. Framed as what the customer gets clarity on.
3. **The decision rule.** Agreed before testing: what result means go, adjust, or stop. Naming it in advance is what keeps the decision honest, because a bar set after seeing the data is a bar that moves.
4. **Step two in full.** One idea, one flow, the one that matters. What "real enough to test" means, and what gets left out on purpose.
5. **The decision brief.** What it contains and how to read it.
6. **The testing playbook.** Where to find the right people, how many you actually need (fewer than expected), how to run sessions so politeness doesn't pollute results, what to measure. Included as guidance. Done-for-you as an add-on.
7. **After the decision.** The three futures: continue, pivot (and specifically what to change), or stop (and why it's a real stop, not "try harder").
8. **CTA.**

Every section answers "what do you get from this step," not "what happens on my end."

---

## Page 3: What I've built

**Job:** proof of shipping, reframed as proof of judgment. This converts the skeptic.

1. **Intro.** "Real apps, built for real ideas. Here's the thinking behind them, not just the screenshots."
2. **Two or three apps as short case notes.** For each: what it is in one line, the problem it solves, **what I'd change if this were a paid product**, and **what I'd kill.** The change and kill notes are the point.
3. **CTA.**

Choose for judgment shown, not technical impressiveness.

## Page 4: About (secondary messaging lives here)

1. **Who I am, briefly.** 20+ years in product and design.
2. **Why I work this way.** The gap between "designed" and "real," why closing it changed what a founder can be offered, and why the honesty matters.
3. **How I work.** Small, scoped, real. Honest about where the value stops.
4. **CTA.**

Human, not a résumé. Even here, tie each point to what it means for the reader.

---

## Page 5: Start

1. **Heading.** "Let's talk about your idea."
2. **What the call is.** Short, no pressure: you share the idea, you hear honestly whether this fits and what step one would look like for you specifically. Free, 20 to 30 minutes.
3. **The booking mechanism.** Scheduler embed or a minimal form (name, one line on the idea, email). The idea line warms the call and qualifies the lead.
4. **Reassurance line.** "If it's not a fit, you'll hear that too, and get pointed somewhere better."

## User flows

### Flow A: the referred lead (highest intent)

```
Home → Hero → skims How it works → CTA → Start → books
```
The Hero must carry the whole offer. This person may book without scrolling.

### Flow B: the skeptic (needs proof)

```
Home → Why you can trust the answer + Proof → What I've built
     → the change/kill notes convince them → CTA → Start → books
```
The proof page does the real work here.

### Flow C: the scared founder (core buyer)

```
Home → The problem + You might be thinking (feels seen)
     → What your life looks like after (relief)
     → How it works (step one checks if it's even worth building)
     → You shouldn't need me twice (trust lands)
     → FAQ → CTA → Start → books
```
Sections 2, 3, 4, 7, and the FAQ are load-bearing for this person.

### Flow D: the ready buyer

```
Any page → persistent CTA → Start → books
```
The CTA must be identical and present on every page and scroll position.

### Flow E: the poor fit (self-selects out)

```
Home → Who this works for → recognizes themselves in the "probably isn't it" list → leaves
```
This is a success, not a loss. Every bad-fit call avoided is time back.

## Notes and decisions still open

- **Headline.** Three options in the Hero. Pick one, or mix.
- **Naming the assets.** "Working build," "decision brief," "testing playbook" are placeholders. Named things feel like property, so it's worth picking names you'd actually say out loud.
- **Naming the offer itself.** A productized sprint benefits from a name. Optional, but it makes referral easier ("he does this thing called X").
- **Exact prices.** Step one as a small standalone fixed price. Full sprint around EUR 3,500 to 5,000 once case studies exist, with a lower launch rate for the first few clients. Decide whether to publish a number or quote on the call.
- **How far up-market to aim.** A funded founder pays the higher number without blinking and expects more polish. A bootstrapper needs the lower one and values the "or stop" more. Both are viable. Pick one to start, because the copy tone shifts between them.
- **Standalone site vs. section under Digital Vitamins.** Spec assumes standalone.
- **Which apps go on the proof page.** Two or three, chosen for judgment.
- **Free intro call vs. selling step one directly.** Spec assumes the free call first.

## A path for later, not now

Once several sprints are done, the repeated parts (the qualifying questions, the decision rule, the testing playbook) start to look like a product rather than a service. That's a real future option: the method, packaged so people can run it without you.

It is not a now option. It needs real client work first, or it's just a theory in a nice wrapper. Noted so it isn't forgotten, and deliberately parked.
